Abstract 
Background
Education, foreign direct investment (FDI), and trade openness 
influence environmental sustainability through competing human 
capital, technology transfer, scale, composition, and pollution-haven 
channels. Evidence from Sub-Saharan Africa remains fragmented and 
often overlooks cross-sectional dependence and asymmetric 
responses.
Methods
This study examines a panel of Sub-Saharan African economies from 
1990 to 2019, using production-based and consumption-based carbon 
dioxide emissions as environmental outcomes. The empirical strategy 
applies cross-sectional dependence and slope-heterogeneity 
diagnostics, second-generation unit root and cointegration tests, 
dynamic seemingly unrelated regression, continuously updated fully 
modified and bias-corrected estimators, asymmetric decompositions, 
and panel causality tests.
Results
Education is consistently associated with lower emissions across the 
principal long-run specifications. FDI is associated with lower 
production-based emissions but higher consumption-based emissions 
in the principal symmetric estimates, while the asymmetric findings 
show that the response differs between positive and negative FDI 
shocks and across time periods. Trade openness is predominantly 
emission-increasing, and urbanisation is associated with higher

Introduction
The ecological status of the planet has never been more susceptible to sustainable management practices than it is at this
moment in the history of modern society, facing an emergency of unparalleled scale. It is a common occurrence at all
levels, and education can be a good channel to mitigate it ( Kumar et al., 2023). The instability and value of the financial
rapport between educational pursuits and environmental sustainability explain the need to establish a more sustainable
future for the world. The crisis of CO 2 emissions into the atmosphere is one of the most significant problems of the
twenty-first century that affects economic growth, health, and environmental sustainability on a global scale. The major
factors of carbon dioxide emissions are anthropogenic, especially deforestation, industrial activities, and burning fossil
fuels (Lone & Bhat). It is clear that emissions from the burning of fossil fuels are one of the primary sources of greenhouse
gases (GHGs) and that the emission of CO2 into the atmosphere contributes to global warming, storms that have increased
in number, rising sea levels, and disrupted ecological stability, which are substantial contributors to climate change
(Soeder, 2021). These changes are not limited to environmental impact but also influence the social and economic fields.
Furthermore, because the climate system is interconnected, the carbon dioxide emissions in a specific area will inevitably
have an effect in other regions; the CO 2 in the atmosphere affects all countries, regardless of the political boundaries.
In turn, it is crucial to understand that the effects of domestic emissions go global, and such phenomena as carbon leakage
also need to be considered ( Pan et al., 2020 ). To start solving the environmental concern of excess carbon dioxide
emissions, nations must examine the relationships between carbon emissions, foreign direct investment, trade openness,
and education.
The complex factors underlying carbon dioxide emissions are closely intertwined with economic, ecological, and social
processes. The constituent components that contribute to the overall carbon footprint do not make equal contributions.
Among them, education (EDU) ( Afolayan et al., 2020 ) and foreign direct investment (FDI) ( Huang et al., 2022 ) are the
most relevant. The other impact of emissions can be influenced by capital flows, such as FDI, which are closely related to
increased economic activity. Through educational efforts, much has been done to advance society ’s trustworthiness and
progress towards greater sustainability. There are also preconditions for the intensification of emissions due to industrial
and commercial growth, trade openness (TO) ( Jun et al., 2020 ), and economic growth (EG) ( Wang et al., 2022 ). Their
energy mix significantly influences countries ’ emission profiles; countries with resources tend to use fossil fuel.
Urbanisation (UR) can boost economic growth but can also increase energy use ( Ali et al., 2020 ). The ecological
footprint (EF) (Ali et al., 2020) and technological development (TD) (Xu and Lan, 2023) are also represented as emission
patterns. The regulations (RQ) and institutional structures (IP) of a specific country determine its ability to develop and
adopt policies that enhance environmental conditions ( Huang et al., 2022 ; Ali et al., 2020 ). Although the effect of
outwards foreign direct investment (OFDI) on a host country is disputed (some researchers may find potential advantages
in the form of economic growth and stabilisation in the case of influence on main spheres,Zafar et al., 2022), its influence
on emissions remains unclear. Therefore, the current analysis asks whether it (EDU) is the mechanism for environmental
sustainability and whether FDI and trade openness (TO) are. Investing in education, FDI, and trade liberalisation are
interconnected and aim to overcome the difficulties faced by scholars and policymakers ( Aust et al., 2020 ). Recent
literature shows that investment in education enhances environmental safety, and FDI and openness to trade can
contribute to environmental degradation ( Tiba and Belaid, 2020 ). Providing more evidence, education (EDU) has
become a determinant, as it has an influential effect on society and the economy.

et al. (2020) and Xu and Lan (2023) examined the relationship between education and CO 2 emissions. Education can

and production growth may increase energy demand. Zafar et al. (2022) emphasised the role of education in adopting
modern energy-efficient technologies. FDI can also increase emissions through scale and pollution haven effects or
reduce them through cleaner technology and management practices (To et al., 2019; Hanh et al., 2020; Hao et al., 2021).
Trade openness can stimulate industrialisation and transport activity while facilitating access to cleaner inputs and

However, free trade facilitates the transition to sustainable finance and demonstrates environmentally friendly technologies. Wang and Zhang (2021) recognised that trade policies can mitigate the effects of emissions because they influence
traded goods. The looming environmental issues in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) have heightened the urgency of this
research. Economic growth and environmental protection must be delicately balanced because the region is focused on
economic growth. The main predictors of this balance are education, foreign direct investment (FDI), and openness to
trade. The interaction between these factors is the focus of the analysis, which is presented in detail to enhance policy
quality. This study is anticipated to fill gaps in the literature by critically analysing the relations using advanced
econometric tools and thus provide subtle and precise information.
Although empirical studies on environmental sustainability are increasingly numerous, research findings on the roles of
education, foreign direct investment (FDI), and openness to trade remain dispersed and fragmented. The literature
documents the emission-cutting and emission-enhancing effects of these organisations, and this lack of certainty hinders
policymakers from developing consistent policies that can balance development and environmental conservation,
particularly in developing nations. Educationists and environmentalists who have researched the convergence between
education and environmental sustainability have arrived at various conclusions. Some researchers have suggested that
more education results in increased consumption and activity among people, thereby contributing to greater carbon
emissions. According to other studies, education enhances environmental awareness, the adoption of cleaner technologies,
and the overall level of public support for environmental protection, leading to decreased pollution. These are mutually
conflicting arguments that are rarely addressed in a single empirical model, and most studies assess education alone
without considering other macroeconomic variables. The same discrepancies prevail in the foreign direct investment (FDI)
literature. The pollution haven hypothesis assumes that FDI leads to increased emissions because more polluting industries
are transferred to countries with weaker environmental regulations. The pollution halo hypothesis, on the other hand, tends
to focus on the movement of technology, efficiency gains, and the spread of superior management practices, leading to
lower pollution. Each of the two positions is supported by empirical data, often based on sample selection, estimation
techniques, or control variables. However, it is not the subject of most research to determine the interactions among the
environmental impact of FDI, human capital, and trade exposure.
The results for trade openness are also conflicting. The scale effect presupposes greater emissions because increased
production and transportation are expected. The effects of composition and technique imply that openness may lower
emissions by shifting production towards cleaner industries and spreading advanced technologies. This is because, in
most empirical literature, the mechanisms are tested individually and based on average effects, which conceal the
possibility of asymmetries and context-dependent effects.
The main literature gap is the lack of an integrated analytical approach that simultaneously examines education, FDI, and
trade openness and addresses issues of heterogeneity, asymmetry, and cross-sectional dependence. These mostly used
single-equation models or first-generation panel estimators, resulting in studies that captured complex interdependent
effects within countries and identified both positive and negative effects. Subsequently, conflicting results persist, and
policy interpretations are poor.
This study aims to fill the gap in the literature by combining education-based human capital, foreign direct investment,
and trade openness for environmental sustainability. The framework explicitly leaves room for asymmetric responses and
second-generation panel methods for long-run dynamics analysis. Thus, this study balances theoretical debates and
provides additional evidence on the circumstances in which education, FDI, and trade can contribute to environmental
enrichment or degradation. This strategy is especially applicable to sub-Saharan Africa, where rapid economic
integration and human capital development are occurring alongside rising environmental pressures.
Literature review and hypothesis development
Theoretical foundation
Environmental sustainability is a discourse that is becoming increasingly vital and will continue to gain momentum in the
future. It expresses the urgency of being efficient in marshalling resources while simultaneously protecting the natural
ecosystems on which humanity depends. In its conventional sense, environmental sustainability (ES) refers to efforts to

ensure that the current generation can fulfil its needs today and that future generations will have the capacity to fulfil their
needs. This knowledge is grounded in the broader concept of sustainable development, which requires the incorporation
of environmental stewardship, social equity, and economic development. Natural resources and ecosystems play a central
role in society ’s welfare, and their assessment is directly linked to the community ’s overall well-being ( Ziaul & Wang,
2023).
Empirical studies provide conceptually consistent explanations of the determinants of ES and are deeply rooted in
economic theory. Human Capital Theory teaches us that the more knowledge a person is provided with, the better
prepared they are to handle the environment. A highly educated population is thus better acquainted with sustainable
practices and is willing to adopt new environmental protection strategies positively ( Mourad, Wahid, Alkubise, and
Najar, 2023 ). The neoclassical EKC theory argues that economic growth does not inevitably lead to environmental
degradation, as long as enlightened citizens steer countries toward cleaner technologies and a more sustainable path of
development. Studies conducted by Duodu et al. show how education can influence attitudes and behaviour to facilitate
civic participation in environmental campaigns, as explained in terms of behavioural economics ( Ekins, 2011).
Environmental sustainability is affected both positively and negatively by trade openness. The scale effect means that as
industrial production and trade increase, pollution and resource consumption rise (Varghese, 2024). On the other hand, a
change in the composition of the environment can be observed as a shift to cleaner industries or services producing what
has been called the environmental composition effect. Rehman, Bhatti, Kraus, and Ferreira (2021) contend that stringent
environmental standards might complement trade, and as a result, a race to the top may mobilise, in which high-income,
environmentally neutral goods are swapped. There is an elegant correlation between ES and foreign direct investment
(FDI). Duodu, Kwarteng, Oteng-Abayie, and Frimpong (2021) propose the Pollution Haven Theory, which suggests that
multinational companies will set up in jurisdictions with weak environmental policies, thereby increasing pollution in the
area. Conversely, the Pollution Halo Hypothesis states that FDI may also be beneficial to developing states by introducing
new technology and improving environmental management systems. Governance plays a critical role, since host-country
regulations are the main drivers of the net effect of FDI on sustainability.
Urbanisation presents both opportunities and challenges. Rapid urban development increases per capita pollution and
resource exploitation, particularly in poorly planned cities (Bordun, Antonyak, Dumych, Hrytsyshyn, and Bilous, 2023).
However, cities have economies of scale that help enhance social services and minimise waste in infrastructure (Bowles,
Boetto, Jones, and McKinnon, 2018 ). City centres of higher learning and innovation encourage sustainability activities
and nurture favourable laws and cultures. However, these objectives should be achieved through sustainable urban
planning and policy frameworks ( Ang, Choolani, and Poh, 2024 ). The four factors connected to education, trade,
openness, and urbanisation interact to form complicated systems that impact environmental sustainability. These
dynamics are important for identifying viable ways to support the provision of ecosystem services. Future governance
should adopt technologies that can realise these strategies.
 Education and environmental sustainability
Education is a catalyst for growth and prosperity, as it is an investment in individuals and society in general. It has long
been known to be an important factor in human development and economic growth. In recent years, scholars have
received growing interest in two-way communication between the environment and education. Further elaboration of this
relationship shows that it is a prerequisite for sustainability in practice, technological innovation, policy advocacy,
diversifying the economy, and exchanging best practices globally. The procedure highlights the immense ability of
education to influence cognition and create a globally conscious society that is sustainably aware of everyone. However,
two empirical studies present opposing results: the first shows that education has a positive relationship with emissions,
and the other indicates the opposite.
The quantitative data for the first group illustrate that education and CO 2 emissions are positively correlated. Afolayan
et al. (2020) concluded that a high level of education allows the economy to be active, which in turn pushes the size of
aggregate emissions. Similarly, Xu and Lan (2023) found that both education and economic growth positively impact
CO2 emissions. Continuous air pollution may be explained by the further use of fossil fuels.Suman (2021) suggested that
the prevalence rates of greenhouse gases depend on the level of education and job opportunities at the regional level.
Moreover, Zafar et al. (2022) hypothesised that education is also one of the major causes of environmental degradation.
The latter group highlights the adverse relationship between education and CO2 emissions. Leal Filho et al. (2019) noted
that energy sustainability is a top-priority agenda among universities in an effort to limit carbon emissions and lower
energy use in buildings. These sustainability programs require huge investments by many institutions of higher learning.
As illustrated byYin, Jiang, Lin, and Liu (2022), online education has a twofold effect on carbon emissions: online classes

emissions through the use of devices and servers. Chen et al. (2022) argued that education enables personal adaptation to

education positively affects carbon emission reduction.
 Foreign direct investment and environmental sustainability
Two different dichotomies have been developed in the literature with respect to the nexus between foreign direct
investment (FDI) and greenhouse gas emissions. An extensive body of literature records a favourable relationship, that is,
increased FDI inflows are correlated with emissions of carbon dioxide. According to Yuan Wang and Huang (2022) ,a
positive initial shock in short-term increases in FDI can trigger an increase in CO 2. Expanding on the pollution haven
hypothesis, Huang et al. (2022) argue that FDI is more concentrated in jurisdictions where environmental regulations are
relatively lenient, thus adding up to 525 percentage points of emissions by 2100, depending on geographic and
institutional factors. According to Hao et al. (2021) , this effect is explained by the emissions produced during different
levels of production and consumption. It is important to mention that the impact of the environment is considerably
softened by the investor sphere, technology, and motivation. Huang et al. (2022) emphasise the role of macroeconomic
variables, namely economic development, financial development, urbanisation, and tourism, in mediating the indirect
impact of FDI on carbon emissions. The (quantitative) relationship was measured by Wang et al. (2022) , and the
coefficient estimates the result of a 1% increment of FDI resulting in a 0.03% increment in emissions. However, the error
correction coefficient has a negative value, which means that, in the short term, the system aims to return the equilibrium
situation to its original condition.
However, a substantial body of research suggests that FDI may lead to technological spillovers that reduce carbon
emissions. To et al. (2019) showed that FDI contributes to the spread of clean technology innovations, thereby helping
prevent emissions. They argue that host countries with higher education levels disproportionately benefit from the
environmental returns of such inflows. Q. The same argument was supported by Wang et al. (2023), who reported that in
better-quality countries, there is a higher likelihood of a reduction in the level of CO2 emissions that can be linked to FDI.
Hanh et al. (2020)found that FDI tends to reduce pollution, primarily by providing environmentally friendly technologies
that decrease energy consumption and carbon emission intensities. As stated byApergis, Pinar, and Unlu (2023)and Ren,
Yuan, Ma, and Chen (2014), the potential of FDI to promote the proliferation of clean technology is decisive in mitigating
greenhouse gas emissions; thus, policy frameworks aimed at capitalising on positive externalities are critical.
 Trade openness and environmental sustainability
Jun et al. (2020) postulate that high levels of trade openness may strengthen the dominance of specific industries, thereby
leading to higher pollution. Chhabra, Giri, and Kumar (2023) revealed that the production of goods is an indicator of
carbon emissions. Additionally, the exchange of products and services can significantly impact the adoption of
environmentally friendly technologies, helping countries transition to more sustainable economic activities. Nonetheless,
when goods are produced using contaminated products, environmental degradation occurs. There is even a possibility
that trade openness can reduce carbon emissions in wealthier nations, as described by Wang and Zhang (2021) .
Furthermore, Ali et al. (2020) found that liberal trade policies can predispose an environment to degradation because
nations may loosen their regulations in the pursuit of trade and investment. Liberalisation forces energy-intensive
industries to relocate to places with less stringent environmental regulations, thereby increasing carbon emissions. Two
distinct claims support this relocation trend, suggesting a common underlying mechanism. Another academic research
stream has introduced a negative dependent correlation between trade openness and carbon dioxide levels. Several
reports argue that liberalised trade may reduce carbon emissions in developed nations. For example,Karedla, Mishra, and
Patel (2021) found that CO 2 emissions in India decreased significantly as the country opened up to international trade.
Accordingly, Lin and Umetsu noted that carbon dioxide emissions are expected to decline in newly industrialised
countries as they become more open to trade. According to Chhabra, Giri, and Kumar (2022) , increased trade facilitates
the exchange of knowledge and technology, thereby improving environmental performance by reducing carbon dioxide
emissions. Finally, Thi, Tran, and Nguyen (2023) argued that emissions can be reduced by utilising renewable energy
sources, increasing trade openness, and fostering innovation. A summary of the literature is provided in Table 1.

Methods
Study design, data and variables
This study uses annual panel data for Sub-Saharan African economies from 1990 to 2019. The reported estimation sample
is a balanced panel of 50 cross-sectional country units observed for 30 years, yielding 1,500 country-year observations
(Table 12). Units were retained when the variables required for the final specifications were available during the study
period. The phrase ‘top 50 polluted nations ’ in the earlier draft was inconsistent with the stated regional scope and has
therefore been removed. The operational definitions, transformations, and original data sources for environmental
outcomes, education expenditure, FDI, trade openness, urbanisation, and gross capital formation are reported in Table 2.

The first coefficient ( β1) captures the marginal association between the share of government expenditure allocated to
education (EDU) and CO2 emissions, holding other regressors constant. EDU is interpreted as a fiscal-input proxy for the
priority assigned to education and the capacity to finance schools, teachers, curricula, and environmental learning. It is not
equivalent to educational attainment, literacy, average years of schooling, education quality, or human capital stock. A
negative coefficient is consistent with an indirect channel in which sustained public education expenditure supports
environmental awareness and the capacity to adopt cleaner practices. However, the coefficient should not be interpreted
as the effect of an additional year of schooling or as a direct change in individual knowledge.
The second coefficient in the equation, 02, represents the exact effect of a unit change in trade openness (TO) on future
variations in CO2 emissions, assuming that all other factors are constant. Variability may also be present in the sign of 2.

implying an increase in emissions, would be a reasonable outcome ( Chhabra et al., 2023; Thi et al., 2023). However, β2

are enhanced by increased openness ( Q. Wang & Zhang, 2021 ; Jun et al., 2020 ).

Estimation and interpretation
The findings from the cross-sectional dependency test ( Table 3) indicate significant cross-sectional dependence among
the variables in the dataset. This implies that the observations are not independent and may exhibit correlations or
interdependence between them. The test results, including the LMBP, LMPS, LMadj, and CDPS statistics, indicate
statistically significant values, suggesting heteroscedasticity and cross-sectional dependence. These findings underscore
the importance of considering the interrelationships between variables when analysing data. Moreover, the test statistics
for Δ and Adj.Δ are statistically significant at the 1% level, suggesting heterogeneity in the research variables.
Panel unit root tests were conducted on the following variables: CO2, C-CO2, education (EDU), foreign direct investment
(FDI), trade openness (TO), and urbanisation (UR). Two specifications were used: one with a constant term and the other
with both a constant and trend. CADF and CIPS test statistics were employed. The results inTable 4 indicate that when the
level specification includes a constant term, the variables exhibit unit roots, but the values are not statistically significant.
However, when we examine the first-difference specification with a constant term, all variables exhibit statistically
significant differences.
The study employed a panel cointegration test to document the long-run association between ES, EDU, TO, FDI, and UR
by applying cointegration techniques familiarised by Westerlund (2007) , Pedroni (2004) , and Kao (1999) . Table 5
presents the test statistics for the different cointegration tests and shows that all tests reject the null hypothesis of no
cointegration. Thus, it is confirmed that there is a long-run association between the variables.

Based on the Granger causality test results in Table 9, a network of causal relationships emerges among the variables.
Education (EDU) is found to Granger-cause carbon emissions (CO 2), indicating a unidirectional causal influence from
education to carbon emissions. Likewise, carbon emissions Granger-cause urbanisation (UR), suggesting a one-way
causal linkage from CO 2 emissions to UR. Foreign direct investment (FDI) Granger-causes trade openness (TO),
indicating a unidirectional causal impact from FDI to TO. Additionally, there are bidirectional causal relationships
between CO2 and trade openness (TO), CO2 and FDI, EDU and TO, EDU and FDI, EDU and UR, TO and UR, and FDI
and UR, indicating mutual causality between these variable pairs. These causal relationships shed light on the complex
interplay between education, economic factors, and urbanisation in influencing carbon emissions and environmental
sustainability.

The contrast between production- and consumption-based CO2 is substantively significant. Production-based emissions
assign carbon to the territory where production occurs, whereas consumption-based emissions reallocate the embodied

emissions embodied in imported inputs and consumption. This divergence is consistent with carbon leakage and prevents
a simple conclusion on pollution halo. The positive TO coefficients under both accounting measures suggest that scaleand trade-related carbon effects dominate the cleaner technique channel on average in the reported sample.
Table 11 shows stable signs across the alternative estimators. EDU is negative for both production- and consumptionbased emissions, with the largest absolute estimate under CCEMG for production-based CO 2 (/C00.231). FDI, trade
openness, and urbanisation are positive in the CS-ARDL, CCEMG, and AMG specifications. The larger consumptionbased FDI estimates are consistent with the embodied emissions associated with import- and trade-linked consumption.
These results support the core directional findings while demonstrating that the choice of estimator affects magnitude.
The negative error correction coefficient (approximately/C00.43) indicates convergence toward the long-run equilibrium
after a short-run disturbance. The CS-ARDL captures dynamic adjustment while augmenting the model for common
cross-sectional factors. The CCEMG allows for heterogeneous slopes in the presence of common factors. Finally, the
AMG accommodates heterogeneous country coefficients and an unobserved common dynamic process. The agreement
in coefficient signs across these estimators indicates that the principal associations are not confined to one estimation
method, although the remaining observational limitations warrant cautious interpretation.
Endogeneity assessment using panel System-GMM as a robustness check
Table 12 reports the results of the system-GMM robustness check. DSUR, CUP-FM, CUP-BC, CS-ARDL, CCEMG, and
AMG address cross-sectional dependence, common factors, dynamic adjustment, or slope heterogeneity, but they do not
eliminate reverse causality by themselves. The System-GMM complements these estimators by including the lagged
dependent variable and using internal lag instruments for potentially endogenous regressors. The significant lagged
emissions coefficients confirm persistence. The AR(1) results are expected in first-differenced errors, while the nonsignificant AR(2) tests and Hansen p-values indicate that the reported moment conditions and over-identifying
restrictions are not rejected. The instrument counts (29 and 31) remain below the number of cross-sectional units
reported for the panel. These diagnostics improve confidence in the robustness of the associations, although they do not
convert observational designs into definitive causal evidence.

Discussion
The education expenditure proxy is negatively associated with both production-based and consumption-based CO 2
emissions in the principal estimates. This finding is consistent with the fiscal capacity channel, in which sustained public
education spending can support environmental literacy, technology absorption, and the demand for cleaner policies.
Because EDU measures expenditure share rather than attainment or realised human capital, the result should be
interpreted as an association with public educational commitment, not as direct evidence that an additional year of
schooling reduces emissions.
The rising educational levels of people in sub-Saharan Africa contribute to the knowledge of implementing sustainable
practices through positive examples ( Ozbay and Duyar, 2022 ). The transfer of information is vital for spreading green
practices across industries, such as the agricultural and power generation sectors. In addition, education enhances
economic diversification and the de-industrialisation of polluting economic activities. Those with higher knowledge and
experience tend to work in low-carbon industries. High-level education plays a crucial role in developing environmentally conscious industries, including the service sector, high-technology industry, and knowledge economy. The level of

education an individual possesses directly determines their ability to promote strong environmental protection policies
(Iorember, Jelilov, Usman, Isik, & Celik, 2021 ). Based on professional levels, better-educated practitioners can argue in
favour of environmentally friendly policies and regulations at all levels of government. Advancements in reducing CO 2
emissions have been impressive, and to some extent, this achievement can be explained by the effective lobbying that led
to the implementation of stringent environmental rules, stimulation of environmentally friendly energy sources, and
establishment of ambitious emission-cutting objectives ( Eyuboglu & Uzar, 2021 ; Piao & Managi, 2023 ).
The primary purpose of environmental education programs is to educate students about current environmental issues and
encourage them to lead sustainable lives. Learners of formal education programs often engage in environment-protecting
activities, such as volunteerism, environmental awareness, and conservation ( Thor & Karlsudd, 2020 ; Boca & Saracli,
2019; Jadhav, Jadhav, and Raut, 2014). Environmental sustainability education raises awareness of the need to conserve
natural resources and influences potential action. By incorporating the idea of economic, social, political, and ecological
interdependence of urban and rural environments, education can enhance knowledge and motivation, thereby fostering
the competence, ideas, and skills required to preserve and improve natural environments ( Liu, Gong, and Chen, 2018 ).
Both formal and informal educational programs can alter the attitudes and practices of individuals, communities, and
nations toward the environment (Saraclı,Y ılmaz, and Arslan 2014). Education for global competency may shape future
generations into concerned and active participants in solving global social, political, economic, and environmental issues.
Because of its importance in achieving sustainability goals, education is emphasized throughout the 2030 Agenda for
Sustainable Development, which urges all nations to “ensure, by 2030, that all learners acquire the knowledge and skills
needed to promote sustainable development, including, among others, through education for sustainable development
and sustainable lifestyles, human rights, gender equality, promotion of a culture of peace and non-violence, global
citizenship, and appreciation of diversity and inclusion. Policies that support and promote sustainability education are
crucial for integrating climate change and sustainable development into Indonesia ’s educational system. These policies
may foster a shared understanding of sustainability, establish guidelines for integrating sustainability concepts into
educational programs, and support educators ’ professional development ( Abdullah, Zakaria, & Razman, 2018 ).
Trade openness is positively associated with production- and consumption-based CO 2 emissions across the DSUR,
CUP-FM, and CUP-BC estimates in Tables 6 and 7. This pattern supports the scale-effect interpretation: expanded
production, transportation, and trade-linked consumption increase emissions. Nevertheless, the asymmetric results show
that the response differs between expansions and contractions, and between the short and long run. Cleaner technology
diffusion remains a plausible mechanism, but it does not dominate the average coefficient sign reported here.
This study shows that the damages of carbon dioxide emissions can be overcome by increasing openness to trade in
various ways. High trade rates can mitigate carbon dioxide emissions as a higher proportion of actors learn to use less
polluting technologies and share and access more sustainable materials. This is possible because an increase in bilateral
trade may help increase production efficiency, thus reducing the impact on the environment (Rahman, Saidi, and Mbarek,
2020). Weaker trade barriers are associated with lower CO 2 emissions, mediated by a greater level of employment,
moderated financial flows, and a more competitive business environment. The rationale for its functioning is the open
market system that motivates companies to use cleaner technology. If the adoption of such technology results in an
increase in efficiency and output, the economic cost of the environment is likely to decrease ( Cherniwchan, Copeland,
and Taylor, 2017; Sun et al., 2019). The reduction in carbon dioxide emission has been correlated with trade liberalisation
in high- and upper-middle-income economies. Individual nations that have developed technology and environmental
management are able to reduce the harmful implications of their economies on the natural environment, hence acquiring
the world at the technological level. These standards and technologies can be diffused with the help of trade liberalisation,
and other countries can reduce their environmental effects ( Farhani, Chaibi, & Rault, 2014 ; Pao and Tsai, 2010 ).
The environmental association of FDI depends on emissions measures and specifications. In the principal symmetric
estimates, FDI is negatively associated with production-based emissions and positively associated with consumptionbased emissions. Alternative estimators and System-GMM yield positive FDI coefficients, whereas the GHG robustness
specification yields negative coefficients. This variation is consistent with competing pollution havens, pollution halos,
scales, and embodied carbon channels. This also indicates that the study should not characterise FDI as uniformly
beneficial or harmful; the net association depends on investment composition, host-country regulation, technology
transfer, and where emissions are accounted for.
Conclusions and policy implications
Conclusion
This study examines the dynamic relationships among government education expenditure share, trade openness, FDI,
and environmental sustainability in the reported Sub-Saharan African panel. The education expenditure proxy is

negatively associated with both emissions measures; however, this result should not be interpreted as a direct attainment
effect. Trade openness and urbanisation generally increase emissions. FDI produces mixed evidence: it is negatively
associated with territorial production-based emissions in the principal long-run estimates but is positively associated with
consumption-based emissions and in several alternative specifications. The asymmetric estimates further show that
increases and decreases can generate different short- and long-run responses. These findings support policies that
combine sustained educational investment with environmental screening of FDI, cleaner trade logistics, embodied carbon
monitoring, and country-specific regulatory design.
Policy suggestion
In light of the environmental issues that Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) faces and to harness the opportunities in education,
trade openness, and foreign direct investment (FDI) to improve environmental sustainability, the following policy
formulations are proposed: First, educational programs specifically aimed at fostering environmental awareness and
adopting environmentally sustainable practices should be strengthened. The appropriate approach is to make sustainability a central idea in the mainstream curriculum at all levels, from primary to tertiary education. Policymakers should
introduce better teacher training and create instructional materials that outline the importance of individual environmental
custodianship. Moreover, allocating resources to adult training and education campaigns at the community level may
significantly increase their outreach and efficacy. International development agencies must work closely with local
budgetary mechanisms to secure the funds required to implement educational reforms. The expected result is an informed
populace able to advance sustainable measures at both personal and organizational scales, thereby driving a decrease in
CO2 levels and enhancing environmental standards for all.
Second, advertising and green investments should be increased to counter the environmental impacts of FDI and trade
openness, which are the main determinants of economic growth. Governments should consider implementing FDI laws
that compel foreign investors to adhere to environmental performance standards. This supports the notion that
environmental considerations are systematically considered when making investment decisions. The only solution
would be to require the integration of green technologies and environmentally friendly mechanisms as part of the
investment permits. These regulatory changes should be implemented through the consolidation of enforcement systems
and the involvement of citizens, businesses, and non-bank financial institutions. In addition, trade in environmentally
friendly goods and services through interventions should be promoted to offset the environmental externalities arising
from trade liberalisation on global CO 2 emissions. Through these steps, policymakers will be able to improve economic
growth, minimise environmental degradation, and develop a more balanced developmental paradigm for the future.
These advantages are expected to result in better environmental standards, sustainable economic development, and an
increase in social welfare, which makes these reforms appealing to the political establishment as well as to society.
This study had several limitations. First, EDU is a fiscal-input measure that does not directly capture attainment, literacy,
schooling years, education quality, environmental knowledge, or realised human capital. Second, although UR and GCF
capture the urban scale and domestic investment channels, omitted factors such as energy mix, industrial composition,
regulatory enforcement, institutional quality, and environmental policy may still affect the estimates. Third, DSUR, CUP-
FM, CUP-BC, CS-ARDL, CCEMG, AMG, and System-GMM address different forms of dependence, dynamics,
heterogeneity, and endogeneity; however, the observational design does not establish definitive causality. Fourth, the
panel-average coefficients may conceal country-specific effects despite the heterogeneous panel estimators. Future work
should test alternative education measures, additional lag structures and control sets, country or income group
heterogeneity, and sectoral or bilateral embodied carbon channels. Therefore, the regional and period-specific results
should be generalised with caution.
